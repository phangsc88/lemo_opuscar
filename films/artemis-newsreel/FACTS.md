# Fact register

1. Temple of Artemis at Ephesus was one of the Seven Wonders and drew pilgrims from around the Mediterranean.
Source: UNESCO, Ephesus, https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1018/ . Quotation: “Little remains of the famous Temple of Artemis, one of the ‘seven wonders of the world’ which drew pilgrims from all around the Mediterranean”. The description also calls the sanctuary “one of the largest and most powerful sanctuaries of the ancient world.”
2. The old temple burned in 356 BCE and was rebuilt on a grander scale.
Source: Frank Bigelow Tarbell, A History of Greek Art (1896), section on the later fourth century, Project Gutenberg 4390, mirrored at https://github.com/GITenberg/A-History-of-Greek-Art-With-an-Introductory-Chapter-on-Art-in-Egypt-and-Mesopotamia_4390 . Quotation: “The old Temple of Artemis at Ephesus ... was set on fire and reduced to ruins ... in 356 B.C.”; “The Ephesians rebuilt the temple on a much more magnificent scale”. The film does not repeat the anecdote linking the fire to Alexander's birthday.
3. Nineteenth-century excavation recovered its site and remains.
Same source: J. T. Wood “conducted excavations at Ephesus for the British Museum in 1863–74”; “the site of this temple ... was at last discovered and its remains unearthed”. Narration intentionally avoids an exact discovery year.
4. Little survives today.
UNESCO quotation in 1; surviving-remains photograph is explicitly dated 2012 and is not passed off as a 1920s transfer or an image made today.
5. Engravings and the 1750 plan are historical interpretations, not proven reconstructions or evidence that the temple stood intact when the pictures were made. On-screen labels identify illustration/plan dates. No 127-column, 18-metre or precise dimensional claim is made, since historical sources vary in the temple phase they describe.
